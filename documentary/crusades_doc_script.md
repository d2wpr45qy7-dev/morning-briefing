# The Crusades (documentary script)

## COLD OPEN

In the old city of Jerusalem stands the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, built over the place where Christians believe Christ was crucified, buried, and rose again. For a long time now, the wars fought to reach it have been remembered as a stain on the West: an unprovoked assault by greedy and violent men on a peaceful Muslim world.

The men who fought them understood it differently. To them the Crusades were a response: to centuries of conquest, to an appeal for help from the Christians of the East, and to the loss of the holiest places of their faith. This is that story.

## PART ONE: THE LONG CONQUEST

In the year 600, the lands of the eastern and southern Mediterranean were Christian. Jerusalem, Bethlehem and Nazareth. Antioch, where the followers of Jesus were first called Christians. Alexandria, one of the great patriarchates of the early Church. Carthage, the world of Saint Augustine.

After the death of Muhammad in 632, the armies of the new caliphate came out of Arabia. In 636 they destroyed a Byzantine army at the Yarmouk, and Jerusalem surrendered to the caliph Umar around 637. Egypt fell by 642, Carthage in 698, Spain in 711. Sicily was taken in the course of the ninth century, and in 846 an Arab raiding fleet sailed up the Tiber and sacked the basilicas of Saint Peter and Saint Paul outside the walls of Rome.

By then much of the old Christian world was under Muslim rule. Those who remained Christian became dhimmi, a subject people who paid a special tax to keep their faith.

In 1009 the Fatimid caliph al-Hakim ordered the destruction of the Church of the Holy Sepulchre. His men demolished the church and hacked at the rock of the tomb itself.

That church would become the goal of every crusader.

## PART TWO: THE CALL

By the eleventh century the last great Christian power in the East was the Byzantine Empire. In 1071, at Manzikert in eastern Anatolia, the Seljuk Turks destroyed its army and took the emperor Romanos Diogenes prisoner.

Within a decade the Turks had overrun most of Anatolia, and made Nicaea, where the Nicene Creed had been drawn up, their capital, barely a hundred miles from Constantinople.

The road to Jerusalem had grown dangerous. In 1065 a great company of German pilgrims, by some accounts seven thousand strong and led by Bishop Gunther of Bamberg, was attacked near Ramla and had to fight for days to survive.

In March 1095, envoys of the emperor Alexios Komnenos came before Pope Urban the Second at the Council of Piacenza. A German chronicler, Bernold of Constance, recorded their request.

"An embassy of the emperor of Constantinople came to this synod, and humbly begged the lord pope and all the faithful of Christ to bring him some help against the pagans, for the defense of the holy Church."

In November 1095, in a field outside Clermont in France, Urban preached to a great crowd. He spoke of the suffering of the Eastern Christians and of the holy places in the hands of their enemies, and he called on the knights of the West to stop fighting one another and go to their brothers' aid. According to the chronicler Robert the Monk, the crowd answered with a single cry. Deus vult. God wills it.

## PART THREE: A JUST WAR

Christians had long asked whether a war could ever be just. Saint Augustine, writing six centuries before Clermont, answered that it could, under conditions: a just cause, such as defending the innocent or recovering what had been wrongly taken; a legitimate authority to declare it; and a right intention, to restore peace rather than to win plunder.

The First Crusade was framed in those terms. Its cause was the defense of the Eastern Christians and the recovery of Christian lands. Its authority was the pope, answering the emperor's appeal. And it was preached as an armed pilgrimage. Crusaders took vows, sewed the cross onto their clothing, and were promised the remission of the penance due for their sins.

The old picture of crusaders as landless younger sons out for plunder has not survived the records. The historian Jonathan Riley-Smith found that going on crusade could cost a knight four or five times his annual income. Families mortgaged and sold land to pay for it, and most who survived came home poorer than they left.

## PART FOUR: JERUSALEM

The march east was among the hardest campaigns of the Middle Ages. A popular host that went ahead of the main armies, the so-called People's Crusade under Peter the Hermit, was destroyed by the Turks in 1096. The knights who followed crossed Anatolia under heat, thirst and constant attack.

At Antioch they besieged the city for eight months, through a winter of hunger. They took it in June 1098, and were themselves trapped inside by a great Turkish relief army. Starving and outnumbered, they marched out and won.

In June 1099 the survivors reached Jerusalem. Before the final assault they fasted, and walked barefoot in procession around the walls. On the fifteenth of July they broke into the city.

When they reached the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, which the Byzantines had partly rebuilt, they wept. Godfrey of Bouillon, chosen to rule the city, refused the title of king. He would not, it was said, wear a crown of gold where Christ had worn a crown of thorns. Tradition remembers him as the Defender of the Holy Sepulchre.

## PART FIVE: THE HARDER CASES

There are three episodes that any honest account of the Crusades has to face.

The first is the fall of Jerusalem. The crusaders killed a great many of the city's defenders and inhabitants. Later Muslim writers spoke of seventy thousand dead; modern historians, working from letters written at the time, put the toll in the thousands. It was a massacre.

It was also the rule of war on both sides. A city that surrendered on terms was usually spared. A city that refused terms and was taken by storm was not. When Zengi took the crusader city of Edessa by storm in 1144, its people were slaughtered. When the Mamluk sultan Baybars stormed Antioch in 1268, he killed or enslaved its population, and wrote to the city's absent prince describing what he had done.

The second episode has no such explanation. In the spring of 1096, before the main armies left, bands led by men such as Count Emicho attacked the Jewish communities of the Rhineland, at Speyer, Worms and Mainz, and murdered thousands. The bishop of Speyer protected the Jews of his city and punished the attackers. The archbishop of Mainz sheltered Jews in his own palace until the mob broke in. Fifty years later, when a monk named Radulf preached violence against the Jews, Bernard of Clairvaux went to the Rhineland himself to stop him.

The third is 1204. An army of the Fourth Crusade, in debt to Venice and drawn into Byzantine politics, turned on the Christian city of Constantinople and sacked it. Pope Innocent the Third had forbidden the crusaders to attack Christians, had excommunicated them when they took the Christian city of Zara, and condemned the sack when he learned of it. In 2001, Pope John Paul the Second asked the forgiveness of the Orthodox for it.

Jerusalem in 1099 was harsh by our standards and ordinary by those of its age. The Rhineland and Constantinople were crimes, committed by men who broke the crusade's own rules, and condemned by the Church that had called it.

## PART SIX: SALADIN AND THE LONG DEFENSE

The crusader states held on for almost two centuries, always outnumbered. Edessa fell in 1144. In July 1187, at the Horns of Hattin near the Sea of Galilee, the sultan Saladin destroyed the main crusader army, and in October Jerusalem surrendered to him.

Saladin has been remembered in the West as a model of chivalry, and in Jerusalem he spared the inhabitants from massacre and let those who could pay a ransom go free. Many thousands who could not pay were sold into slavery. And after Hattin, he had the captured Templars and Hospitallers, about two hundred men, put to death in his presence.

The Third Crusade, led by Richard the Lionheart, retook Acre and defeated Saladin at Arsuf in 1191. It was cruel on both sides here too. At Acre, when an exchange of prisoners broke down, Richard had some two thousand seven hundred Muslim captives executed. Richard never retook Jerusalem, but his treaty with Saladin in 1192 secured the right of Christian pilgrims to visit the holy places.

For another century the crusaders held a strip of coast against rising Muslim powers. Antioch fell to Baybars in 1268. In May 1291 Acre, the last great crusader city, fell, and the crusader states of the Holy Land came to an end.

## PART SEVEN: THE MEMORY OF THE CRUSADES

It is often said that the Islamic world has carried the memory of the Crusades as a wound for nine centuries. The record suggests otherwise.

For medieval Muslim writers, these were wars against the Franks, one frontier struggle among many, and one they had won. Arabic had no common term for the Crusades until the nineteenth century, when one was adopted from European usage, and the first Arabic history of the Crusades appeared in 1899.

In Damascus, the tomb of Saladin was neglected for centuries. It was restored in 1898, at the expense of a European: the German emperor Wilhelm the Second, who laid a wreath there during a state visit.

The idea of the Crusades as a great historical crime against Islam took shape largely in the modern era, in European romantic writing and in twentieth-century anti-colonial politics.

## EPILOGUE: LEPANTO AND VIENNA

The conflict of which the Crusades were a part did not end in 1291. In 1453 the Ottoman Turks took Constantinople and turned the great church of Hagia Sophia into a mosque. Their armies advanced through the Balkans and into central Europe.

On the seventh of October, 1571, at Lepanto, off the coast of Greece, the fleet of a Holy League assembled by Pope Pius the Fifth met the Ottoman navy. Its commander was Don Juan of Austria, the man who had put down the Morisco rebellion in Spain. The pope had asked all of Christendom to pray the Rosary for victory. The League won, and the Church still keeps the seventh of October as the feast of Our Lady of the Rosary.

In 1683 an Ottoman army besieged Vienna. On the twelfth of September, the king of Poland, Jan Sobieski, led a great cavalry charge down from the hills above the city and broke the siege. To the pope he sent a message that echoed Julius Caesar: we came, we saw, God conquered.

From the Yarmouk to Vienna, it was more than a thousand years of the same frontier. The Crusades were Christendom's most famous attempt to push it back.

## CLOSING

The Christian lands of the East had been taken by force over four and a half centuries. The church over Christ's tomb had been torn down on a caliph's orders. The last Christian empire in the East had been broken at Manzikert, and its emperor had asked the West for help.

The pope answered with a call to defend the innocent and recover what had been taken, preached as a pilgrimage of penance, under rules of just war worked out by Saint Augustine. The men who answered spent their fortunes to go. When some of them committed crimes, the Church that sent them condemned those crimes.

Not every man who took the cross deserves admiration. But the Crusades cannot be understood without the centuries that came before them, or without the reason most crusaders gave for going: an empty tomb in Jerusalem, and the Church of the Holy Sepulchre, which still stands over it today.
