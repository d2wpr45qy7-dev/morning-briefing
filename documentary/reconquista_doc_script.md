# The Reconquista (documentary script)

## COLD OPEN

In the gardens of the Alhambra, water still runs through channels laid out more than seven hundred years ago. To many visitors, places like this tell a simple story: that Islamic Spain was a golden age of tolerance, where Muslims, Christians and Jews lived side by side, until the Christian kingdoms of the north brought it to an end.

The people who fought those wars told a different story. To them it was not a conquest but a recovery: the reclaiming of a Christian land that had been taken by force, after nearly eight centuries of war. This is that story.

## PART ONE: THE CONQUEST

In 589, at the Third Council of Toledo, the Visigothic king Reccared renounced the Arian creed and brought his kingdom into the Catholic Church. For more than a century afterward, Hispania was a Catholic realm, with its own bishops, its own councils, and its own written law.

In the spring of 711, an army of Arabs and Berbers under the commander Tariq ibn Ziyad crossed the strait from North Africa. The rock where he landed still bears his name. Jabal Tariq, the mountain of Tariq. Gibraltar.

That summer, near the Guadalete river, King Roderic's army was destroyed, and the king was lost with it. Within seven years almost the entire peninsula was under the rule of the Umayyad Caliphate. Only the mountains of the far north remained beyond its reach. The advance into Europe was halted in 732, near Tours, by the Frankish leader Charles Martel.

A Christian chronicler living under the new rulers set down what had happened. Writing in 754, he described cities burned, the leading men condemned to the cross, and young men and infants cut down with daggers. And then he wrote a lament.

"Who could tell of such perils? Who could count such grievous disasters? Even if every limb were turned into a tongue, human nature could never describe the ruin of Spain."

Christians and Jews who remained became dhimmi: protected, but subordinate. They paid the jizya, a tax levied on those who kept their faith. New churches were restricted, worship was kept out of public view, and leaving Islam was punishable by death. In Córdoba in the 850s, forty-eight Christians were executed, some for publicly denouncing Islam, others for being judged apostates from it. The Church remembers them as the Martyrs of Córdoba.

Under the Almohads, a North African dynasty that seized al-Andalus in the twelfth century, conditions grew harsher. Christians and Jews were pressed to convert, to flee, or to die. Among the families who fled was that of the Jewish philosopher Moses Maimonides, who left Córdoba as a boy and never returned.

Centuries later, Spain itself would be condemned for giving its religious minorities a choice between conversion and exile. In al-Andalus, that choice had first been put to Christians and Jews.

## PART TWO: THE LONG ROAD BACK

In the mountains of Asturias, a Visigothic nobleman named Pelayo refused to submit. Around 722, at a place called Covadonga, his men ambushed a Muslim force in a narrow valley and drove it back. The battle was small. Its consequences were not. The kingdom that survived in those mountains grew into León and Castile, while in the Pyrenees other Christian lands took shape, the forerunners of Navarre, Aragon and Catalonia.

What followed was not a single war. It was a frontier that moved over centuries, a castle, a valley, a river line at a time. Castile takes its name from the castles built to hold it.

The raids ran in both directions. In 997 the Córdoban chief minister al-Mansur, known to the Christians as Almanzor, marched on Santiago de Compostela, the shrine of Saint James and one of the great pilgrimage sites of Christendom. He sacked the city, though he left the apostle's tomb untouched, and carried away the bells of its church. According to the chronicles, Christian captives were made to carry them to Córdoba, where they were hung as lamps in the Great Mosque.

In 1212, at Las Navas de Tolosa, the combined armies of Castile, Aragon and Navarre broke the Almohad army. Within a generation most of al-Andalus had fallen. In 1236 Ferdinand the Third of Castile took Córdoba. The chronicles record that he had the bells of Santiago returned to Compostela, this time carried by Muslim captives.

To the kings who fought it, this was restoration: the recovery of land, cities and churches that had once been Christian.

Conquest did not mean the end of the Muslim communities. As the frontier moved south, surrendering towns were usually offered terms. Muslims who stayed under Christian rule, the mudéjares, kept their mosques, their law and their property in return for tribute and loyalty. The door to conversion was always open, and a convert entered Christian society as a full member of the Church. Where the mudéjares rose in revolt, as in Andalusia and Murcia in 1264, they were driven out. Where they kept faith with the Crown, they stayed, in some places for centuries.

## PART THREE: 1492

By the late fifteenth century only the Emirate of Granada remained. Ferdinand of Aragon and Isabella of Castile, whose marriage had united their crowns, fought a ten-year war to take it. On the second of January, 1492, the last Nasrid ruler, Muhammad the Twelfth, known to the Christians as Boabdil, surrendered the keys of the city. After seven hundred and eighty-one years, the peninsula was Christian again.

The terms of surrender were generous. The Capitulations of Granada, agreed in November 1491, guaranteed the city's Muslims their mosques, their property, their law and their religion. No one was required to convert. Those who wished to leave for North Africa could go, and take their goods with them.

The first archbishop of Granada, Hernando de Talavera, the queen's own confessor, chose persuasion. He encouraged his clergy to learn Arabic, had prayers and teaching translated, and won converts by example. The Muslims of the city are said to have called him the holy alfaquí.

In 1499 that changed. Cardinal Francisco Jiménez de Cisneros came to Granada and pressed for faster results, including mass baptisms and the burning of Arabic books. The Muslims of the Albaicín quarter rose in revolt, and the rising spread into the mountains of the Alpujarras. The Crown's position was that the rebellion had voided the treaty.

In 1502 Castile issued its edict. The Muslims of the kingdom were given a choice: accept baptism and remain, or leave. The routes out were narrow, and most were baptized. From that moment, in law, they were Christians, entitled to stay in their homes, keep their land, and live as subjects of the Crown. In Valencia and Aragon the same choice followed in 1525 and 1526.

It was a demand, not an invitation. But it is worth setting beside the choice the Almohads had put to Christians and Jews three centuries earlier. In Castile in 1502 no one was offered death. The way to stay was open, and it ran through the baptismal font.

## PART THREE AND A HALF: THE HARDER CASE

Three months after the fall of Granada, on the thirty-first of March, 1492, Ferdinand and Isabella signed the Alhambra Decree. Every Jew in their kingdoms was to leave by the end of July. Those who accepted baptism could stay.

The Jews of Spain had not been the conquerors of 711. Under the Almohads they had been persecuted alongside the Christians. The case for the Reconquista, the recovery of a conquered land, does not apply to them, and this film does not claim that it does.

The decree gave its own reason, in its opening lines.

"We were informed that in our kingdoms there are, and have been, some bad Christians who judaized from our Holy Catholic Faith, of which the communication of the Jews with the Christians was much to blame."

The Crown's claim was that practicing Jews were drawing the conversos, Jews who had already been baptized, many of them during the violence and mass conversions of 1391, back into Jewish observance. How much of it was true, and how much was suspicion, is still argued over.

Spain was not the first kingdom to expel its Jews. England did so in 1290, and France in 1306 and again in 1394, with no option to stay. Spain's decree left a door open: baptism, and with it the right to remain.

Many took it. Abraham Seneor, the chief rabbi of Castile and one of the most powerful men in the kingdom, was baptized in June 1492, with Ferdinand and Isabella as his godparents. He took the name Fernando Pérez Coronel and remained in royal service. Others, like the scholar and financier Isaac Abravanel, refused and left. Tens of thousands went into exile.

Of all the decisions in this story, this is the hardest to defend. It made sense within the political theology of its age, one realm under one faith, and it was less severe than what England and France had done. But it lacked what the later Morisco case would have: rebellion, foreign alliance, and a war on Spain's own coasts.

## PART FOUR: BEHIND CLOSED DOORS

Baptism made the Moriscos, as the converted Muslims came to be called, Christians in law. Whether it made them Christians in fact was another question, and the Spanish Inquisition, founded in 1478, existed to answer it.

The records show that many kept their old faith in private. Families kept the fast of Ramadan and the ritual washing before Friday prayer. Some washed away the baptismal oil when they returned home. They held Islamic weddings after the Christian ceremony, and avoided pork and wine at every meal, which in a Catholic society was a visible sign. They wrote religious texts in aljamiado, Spanish written in Arabic script, which their neighbors could not read.

In 1504 a Muslim jurist in North Africa, the Mufti of Oran, issued a ruling addressed to the Muslims of Spain. It told them they could attend Mass, bow before the cross, and even eat pork if they were forced to, so long as they held to Islam in their hearts.

To the Inquisition, this was the heart of the matter. The question was never whether a Morisco had been baptized. It was whether the conversion was real. A convert who lived as a Christian was a Christian. One who did not was, in the Inquisition's eyes, a heretic.

## PART FIVE: THE ENEMY AT THE COAST

This was not only a question of conscience. Through the sixteenth century Spain was at war with the Ottoman Empire, the strongest power in the Mediterranean, while the corsairs of North Africa raided the Spanish coast, burning villages and carrying their people off to be sold as slaves.

Some Moriscos aided them, as guides, informants and correspondents of the Ottoman court. In 1568 the Moriscos of the Alpujarras rose in open rebellion and proclaimed a king of their own, Aben Humeya. Volunteers and arms came across the sea from North Africa. It took more than two years, and an army under Don Juan of Austria, to put the rising down. Both sides committed atrocities. Priests and Christian villagers were killed, and Morisco towns were sacked.

After the rebellion, the Moriscos of Granada were deported and scattered across Castile. Suspicion did not fade. In 1605 the authorities claimed to have uncovered a Morisco plot in Valencia, backed by France.

In 1609 Philip the Third ordered the expulsion of the Moriscos. The first proclamation was read in Valencia that September, and the royal letter it contained gave the king's reasons. The Moriscos, he wrote, had persisted in apostasy and treason, and through their ambassadors had sought the harm and disturbance of his kingdoms. Over the next five years, roughly three hundred thousand people were removed from Spain.

## PART SIX: THREE WAYS TO SEE 1609

It was a hard measure, and sincere Catholics were caught up in it. There are three ways to understand it, and an honest history holds all three.

The first is fear. The Spaniards of 1609 saw in the Moriscos the heirs of the people who had taken their country in 711, and they had recent reason to be afraid. The Alpujarras had burned within living memory. The corsairs were still raiding the coast. The Ottoman fleet that was smashed at Lepanto in 1571 had been rebuilt within a year.

The fair answer is that most Moriscos were not the heirs of the Arab and Berber armies. They were mostly descended from native Iberians who had converted to Islam over the centuries, and by around the year 1000, historians estimate, most of al-Andalus was already Muslim, overwhelmingly by conversion rather than migration. Historians still argue over how real the 1605 plot was, and over how many Moriscos in places like Valencia, which never rose in 1568, had any part in the corsairs' war. The fear may have outrun the danger. But it was not invented.

The second is the Church. As an institution, the Church did not condemn the expulsion. Juan de Ribera, the archbishop of Valencia, later canonized, was among its strongest advocates, and saw it as the culmination of the Reconquista. But the Church was not of one mind. Some clergy argued for patient instruction instead, the humanist Pedro de Valencia argued for integration, and the nobles and parliament of Valencia, who stood to lose their workers, appealed to the Crown to stop it, and were refused. The Church's own teaching cut against the decision. The same Church that insisted in 1449 that the baptized are one in Christ was now watching a king remove baptized Catholics from his realm on the suspicion that their baptism was a mask.

And even in 1609, the path of conversion was not wholly closed. The Valencian proclamation itself set out exceptions. Children under four could stay if their parents agreed. Where the father was an Old Christian, his young children and their Morisca mother stayed. In each village, six households in every hundred were to remain, chosen from those who had given the most signs of being Christians. And two clauses spoke directly to faith.

"Likewise, those who for a considerable time, such as two years, have lived among Christians without attending the assemblies of the aljamas. Likewise, those who receive the Most Holy Sacrament with the license of their prelates."

In practice these exceptions were applied unevenly, and many who qualified were expelled anyway. But where a community had truly become part of Christian Spain, the record shows that it could survive. In the town of Villarrubia de los Ojos, in La Mancha, Old Christian neighbors, parish priests and the local lord defended the Moriscos, and many who were expelled came back and stayed.

The third way is the simplest. The Reconquista was justified as a just war: a just cause, a legitimate authority, and the intention of recovering a land that had been taken. That cause was complete in 1492. The expulsion came a hundred and seventeen years later, when no war was being fought on Spanish soil, as a decision of a king about his own subjects. If 1609 went too far, it does not reach back and unwrite the recovery of Spain. Each must be judged on its own terms.

## PART SEVEN: THE ONES WHO STAYED

The clearest evidence of what conversion meant in Spain is in the lives of those who converted and stayed.

Solomon ha-Levi was the chief rabbi of Burgos. Around 1390 he was baptized, and took the name Pablo de Santa María. He became bishop of Burgos, the city where he had led the Jewish community, and served at the royal court as counselor and tutor. His son, Alonso de Cartagena, succeeded him as bishop and represented Castile at the Council of Basel, one of the leading churchmen of his time.

Luis de Santángel came from a converso family. As a finance official of the Crown of Aragon, he helped raise the money for the voyage of Christopher Columbus in 1492.

Hernando de Talavera, the archbishop of Granada, was himself believed to be of converso descent. In the last years of his life, the Inquisition investigated him and arrested members of his household. The case reached Rome, and he was cleared shortly before his death in 1507. His conversion, and his family's, was tested, and it held.

The same door was open to Muslims. Cidi Yahya al-Nayyar, a prince of the Nasrid royal family, converted during the war for Granada and took the name Don Pedro de Granada. He fought for the Crown and held high office in Christian Granada, and his descendants, the Granada Venegas, became Castilian nobility.

Two sons of the emir Abu l-Hasan, half-brothers of Boabdil himself, were baptized as Don Fernando and Don Juan de Granada and lived out their lives as Castilian nobles. In 1593 a prince of the royal house of Morocco, Muley al-Shaykh, was baptized at the Escorial with King Philip the Second as his godfather. He became Don Felipe de África, a knight of the Order of Santiago, the military order founded to fight the Moors.

Alonso del Castillo, a Morisco physician from Granada, became the Crown's Arabic interpreter, served as a translator during the Alpujarras war, and catalogued the Arabic manuscripts of the Escorial. Miguel de Luna, another Morisco physician, served Philip the Second as an interpreter and wrote a widely read history of the fall of Visigothic Spain.

Then there are the saints. The grandfather of Teresa of Ávila, Juan Sánchez of Toledo, was penanced by the Inquisition in 1485 for returning to Jewish practice. He was reconciled, moved his family to Ávila, and his granddaughter became one of the great mystics of the Church and a Doctor of the Church. John of Ávila, also a Doctor of the Church, came from a converso family. So did Diego Laínez, the second superior general of the Jesuits.

The pattern runs through the whole record. Conversion was demanded, and it was tested, sometimes harshly. But where it was real, it held, and those who made it became Spaniards, some of them all the way to the altars.

## PART EIGHT: PURITY OF BLOOD

The sharpest charge against Spain's record is the limpieza de sangre, the purity of blood statutes. Beginning in Toledo in 1449, some cities, cathedral chapters, universities and military orders barred the descendants of converts from their ranks.

Their defenders saw them as a matter of security. By the mid-1400s Spain had hundreds of thousands of conversos, many baptized under pressure after 1391, and the Inquisition kept finding secret Jewish practice among some of them. A baptismal record, they argued, showed that a man had been baptized. It did not show what his household believed.

There was also the question of power. The Toledo statute of 1449 came out of a revolt against royal tax collectors, many of them conversos. A century later, when Archbishop Juan Martínez Silíceo imposed a statute on Toledo's cathedral chapter in 1547, his argument was that the most important church in Spain was filling with men descended from people the Inquisition had condemned. The statutes covered the institutions that guarded the faith and governed the kingdom: cathedral chapters, the Inquisition itself, the great colleges, the military orders.

So the defenders' case was this. They were not saying that converts could not be saved, could not marry, own property, or live as Spaniards. They were saying that the posts guarding faith and state should go to families whose loyalty had been proven over generations, in a country where too many baptisms had turned out to be masks. They called it prudence, not doctrine. Even the Jesuits, whose founder had welcomed converts, eventually adopted a statute in 1593.

The Church never accepted the principle. In 1449, the same year as the Toledo statute, Pope Nicholas the Fifth condemned it, declaring that every baptized Christian is one in Christ. Alonso de Cartagena, the converso bishop, wrote a treatise defending the unity of all the baptized. As the generations passed and the fear of secret Judaism faded, Spaniards themselves turned against the statutes, and the Count-Duke of Olivares tried to rein them in.

The fairest verdict is that the purity statutes were a security measure born of real betrayals, in a real war, by people with real reasons for suspicion, and that by the Church's own teaching they went too far, because they punished sons and grandsons for doubts about their ancestors. And the proof is in the saints. If the statutes had been the whole truth about Spain, there would have been no Teresa of Ávila.

## CLOSING

A Christian country was conquered by force in 711. For centuries its people lived as a taxed and subordinate class in their own land, and at times were given a choice between conversion, exile and death. They fought for nearly eight hundred years to recover it.

As they did, they offered terms. Under the medieval kings, Muslims could stay as Muslims, so long as they kept the peace. After 1492, the Crown demanded more. The path to remaining in Spain ran through baptism, and it was open to Muslim and Jew alike. Many who took it in good faith rose to the highest places in the Church and the kingdom. Many who took it only outwardly, and some who took up arms beside Spain's enemies, were eventually driven out.

The bells of Santiago were carried to Córdoba in 997, and carried home again in 1236. To the Spaniards who rang them, that was what the Reconquista meant: not the conquest of a foreign land, but the recovery of their own.
