# Weekly Essays — August 15, 2026

## Chevron Is Dead. What Killed It Was Also Its Best Argument.
*Politics — Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo and the future of administrative law*

For forty years, Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, 467 U.S. 837 (1984), told courts that when a statute was ambiguous, they should defer to any reasonable interpretation an agency gave it. In June 2024, the Court killed that rule in Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo, 603 U.S. ___, 144 S. Ct. 2244 (2024), 6–3, Chief Justice Roberts writing for the majority. The case arose from something almost comically small — a National Marine Fisheries Service rule forcing herring boats to pay for the federal observers required to ride along and monitor them — and turned into the most significant contraction of administrative power in a generation.

Take Chevron's case seriously first, because it was not stupid. Justice Stevens's original opinion rested on a real institutional insight: agencies have subject-matter expertise courts don't, they are accountable to an elected president in a way life-tenured judges are not, and when Congress leaves a gap or an ambiguity in a regulatory statute, that is often not sloppy drafting but an implicit delegation to the body actually equipped to fill it — the EPA on emissions science, the FCC on spectrum allocation, the SEC on market structure. Forty years of the modern regulatory state were built assuming that framework would hold: businesses, states, and citizens organized their compliance and their litigation strategy around known deference rules. Justice Kagan's dissent in Loper Bright, joined by Justices Sotomayor and Jackson, pressed exactly this point — that the majority was unsettling a stable, functional equilibrium to chase a formalist purity that federal judges, who are generalists, are the ones worst positioned to exploit responsibly.

Roberts's answer is textual and, I think, basically right: the Administrative Procedure Act says courts "shall decide all relevant questions of law" and "interpret constitutional and statutory provisions," 5 U.S.C. § 706 — not "shall defer to the agency's interpretation when reasonable." Chevron never had a real textual home in the APA; it was a judicially invented doctrine of judicial humility that, over four decades, hardened from optional deference into something close to a rule requiring courts to abdicate the interpretive job Article III and the APA actually assign them. The expertise argument, taken to its logical end, proves too much — it would justify deferring to agencies on constitutional questions too, since agencies often have more expertise there as well, and nobody thinks that.

Where the majority opinion gets internally shaky, and where a sharp lawyer on either side should press, is the transition rule it built to avoid chaos: Loper Bright preserves the actual holdings of every case that already relied on Chevron, under ordinary statutory stare decisis, even though the framework producing those holdings no longer exists. That is a strange position to hold at once — the reasoning is dead, but its fruit lives on, and every one of those legacy holdings must now be sorted, case by case, into "would have come out the same way without Chevron" and "was actually driven by deference and might not survive fresh scrutiny," a sorting job with no clean test and that will occupy the lower courts for years. The Court also left Skidmore deference standing — agencies still get a hearing for genuine expertise, just as persuasive authority instead of controlling authority — which is a coherent position but means the practical difference between the old and new regimes will often be smaller than the rhetoric around this decision suggests, decided court by court, statute by statute.

The lawyering incentives shift in a predictable direction. Agency counsel now has to defend rules on the merits of statutory text from the start, which pushes rulemaking preambles toward more explicit textualist argument up front rather than reliance on a deference backstop. Challengers' counsel gains a much stronger tool: find any genuinely ambiguous statutory hook and argue for the better reading de novo, rather than the much harder task of showing an agency's reading was unreasonable. That, combined with the existing practice of filing in favorable single-judge districts, means where you sue is now worth even more than it already was — a court's own priors about regulatory policy do more work when there's no deference floor forcing judges toward the agency's reading regardless of their priors.

**The strongest objection**

Loper Bright claims to restore constitutional first principles, but it doesn't actually solve the underlying problem it diagnoses — it just moves the discretion. Congress still writes ambiguous statutes and still doesn't fill the gaps itself; that discretion now sits with generalist federal judges instead of subject-matter agencies, and judges have no more democratic accountability than agency heads do, arguably less, since agency heads answer to an elected president and Senate-confirmed appointees while Article III judges answer to no one. Swapping expert-agency discretion for judge discretion isn't obviously a win for democratic legitimacy; it might just be a different flavor of the same problem dressed in separation-of-powers language. That's a fair hit, and I don't think Roberts's opinion fully reckons with it. My answer is narrower than a full defense of the outcome: the question Loper Bright actually resolves isn't "who governs better," it's "who has the interpretive authority the APA assigns," and on that narrower textual question the majority has the better reading. Whether courts exercise that authority wisely is a separate problem, and the honest prediction is that it will be exercised unevenly, circuit by circuit, for at least a decade.

Sources: Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S. 837 (1984); Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo, 603 U.S. ___, 144 S. Ct. 2244 (2024); Relentless, Inc. v. Department of Commerce (companion case, 2024); Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 706; Skidmore v. Swift & Co., 323 U.S. 134 (1944); Justice Kagan, dissenting opinion in Loper Bright.

## The Trial of Thomas More Was Unjust. That Doesn't Make Him What the Movie Says He Was.
*History — the 1535 treason trial of Thomas More and the historiographical fight over his legacy*

On July 1, 1535, Thomas More stood trial at Westminster Hall for high treason under the Treasons Act of 1534. He was convicted and beheaded five days later. The trial is a genuine legal scandal by the standards of its own time, and it is also the origin point of a hagiographic legend that later historians have spent a century picking apart — and both things are true simultaneously, which is exactly why the case is worth taking seriously rather than flattening into the story A Man for All Seasons tells.

Start with the legal problem, because it's real and it's precise, not just a vague sense that Tudor justice was harsh. The Act required an overt, malicious denial of the royal supremacy; More had spent over a year deliberately saying nothing, on the calculated legal theory — "qui tacet consentire videtur," silence implies consent, not denial — that silence could not be prosecuted as treasonous speech. The Crown had no public statement to convict him on, so the entire capital case rested on a single alleged private conversation in the Tower on June 12, 1535, between More and Richard Rich, the solicitor general, in which Rich claimed More had said Parliament had no more power to make the king head of the Church than to legislate that "God should not be God." Two other men were in the room — Richard Southwell and More's own servant — and both denied hearing any such statement. It was one uncorroborated witness, of dubious character, against a former Lord Chancellor, on a capital charge. More told the court, on hearing Rich's testimony, that he was "sorrier for your perjury than for my own peril" — and the near-universal judgment of historians since, hostile and sympathetic alike, is that Rich lied, almost certainly with Thomas Cromwell's knowledge. Convicting a man of treason on one uncorroborated, self-interested witness was legally dubious even by 1535 standards, and English law moved specifically against it afterward — the Treason Act of 1547 introduced a two-witness requirement partly in reaction to trials exactly like this one.

That is the part the traditional story gets right. Where it goes wrong is in what it does with More's character once he's cast as the victim of that injustice. The hagiographic tradition — William Roper's Life of Sir Thomas More, written by his son-in-law around 1556, and R.W. Chambers's influential 1935 biography — built More into a nearly unblemished martyr of individual conscience, a reading the 1966 film cemented in popular imagination: a man standing alone against state power on principle. Revisionist historians have spent the decades since complicating that picture hard. As Lord Chancellor from 1529 to 1532, More actively prosecuted heresy with real severity, was involved in cases that sent Protestants to the stake, and wrote polemics against Luther and Tyndale — the Responsio ad Lutherum among them — of a viciousness that sits uneasily next to the humanist wit of Utopia. Richard Marius's 1984 biography draws a psychologically harder portrait of a man capable of real cruelty toward those he considered heretics; the wider current of Tudor revisionism associated with G.R. Elton's scholarship pushed back generally against sympathetic, decontextualized readings of Reformation-era figures, More included. Peter Ackroyd's 1998 biography tries to hold both halves together rather than choosing a side. The dispute among historians is not really about the facts of the trial — those are fairly well settled — it's about how to weigh a man who was, by any fair reading, both the victim of a legally scandalous prosecution and, when he held power, an enthusiastic persecutor of people who held views not unlike the ones he was executed for holding himself, in reverse.

The pre-law point is the sharper one, and it's worth pulling out explicitly: More's trial is a foundational case study in what happens when the executive controls both prosecution and bench, when statutory language ("maliciously") is stretched to cover silence through inference, and when a capital conviction rests on uncorroborated testimony from a witness with an obvious motive to please the Crown. Those specific failures — not "Tudor justice was harsh" in the abstract, but these particular procedural defects — are traceable forward into the corroboration and confrontation protections that Anglo-American criminal law built up over the following two centuries, imperfectly and unevenly, but recognizably in response to cases like this one.

**The strongest objection**

Someone could reasonably say the "unjust trial" framing is itself anachronistic, because it borrows a modern due-process sensibility More himself never held and would not have extended to his own victims — a man who thought heresy deserved burning has no special claim on our sympathy for procedural fairness when the state comes for him instead. That's a fair consistency challenge to More the man, and I'm not interested in rescuing his reputation from it. But it doesn't actually reach the narrower historical claim being made here, which isn't about More's virtue — it's about whether the Crown's own contemporary legal standards, the ones the state itself claimed to be operating under, were violated in this specific case. They were: the single-witness problem and the silence-as-treason theory are defects measured against 1535 law, not against twenty-first-century liberalism imported backward. More's inconsistency as a moral actor and the illegality of his prosecution are two separate questions, and you can hold both — he was unjustly convicted, and he was not the uncomplicated hero the story made him.

Chambers, Thomas More (1935); Richard Marius, Thomas More: A Biography (1984); Peter Ackroyd, The Life of Thomas More (1998); Treason Act 1547 (two-witness requirement); "The Trial of Sir Thomas More," Famous Trials archive, University of Missouri–Kansas City (Douglas O. Linder).

## The August Birthright-Citizenship Orders Are a Bet That Losing Twice Is Cheap
*Conversation starter — Trump v. Barbara and the new August 2026 citizenship executive orders*

On June 30, 2026, the Supreme Court ruled 6–3 in Trump v. Barbara that President Trump's January 2025 executive order stripping citizenship from children born in the U.S. to undocumented or temporary-status parents was unconstitutional. Chief Justice Roberts wrote the majority; Justice Thomas, joined by Justice Gorsuch, and Justice Alito separately, dissented. The majority reaffirmed United States v. Wong Kim Ark, 169 U.S. 649 (1898): the Fourteenth Amendment's Citizenship Clause confers citizenship on children born on U.S. soil who are "subject to the jurisdiction" of the United States, and that phrase carries only the narrow historic common-law exceptions — children of foreign diplomats and of hostile occupying forces — not a broader domicile or lawful-status test the administration wanted read in.

Five weeks later, on August 6, 2026, the President signed two new orders: "Continuing to Protect the Meaning and Value of American Citizenship," which excludes children of foreign diplomats and self-described "alien enemies," and "Ending Birth Tourism," aimed at pregnant visitors on temporary visas. My thesis: the first order isn't really new law, and the second one is a bet that the value of trying again exceeds the cost of losing again — and courts should start treating that bet as evidence of bad faith rather than resetting the clock on it.

Start with what's actually at stake legally, because the two orders aren't the same kind of claim. The diplomat and hostile-occupier exclusions restated in the first order were never in dispute — Wong Kim Ark itself preserved those exact common-law categories in 1898, and Trump v. Barbara didn't touch them. That order is close to symbolic: it announces enforcement of a rule nobody contests. The second order is the live fight, and it's live precisely because the Court's own June opinion seems to foreclose it. If the majority rejected any domicile-or-permanence gloss on "subject to the jurisdiction" — and by its own account it did, exhaustively, canvassing the Clause's text and history and finding no ratification-era support for a status-based limitation — then a temporary visa holder present specifically to give birth is not meaningfully different, under the Court's own reasoning, from any other temporary or undocumented parent whose child was already held to be a citizen in June. The birth-tourism order isn't distinguishing a new category the Court left open; it's re-arguing a category the Court just closed, dressed in narrower language.

That's not necessarily irrational as executive strategy, and this is the part worth sitting with at dinner rather than the doctrine. An administration that expects to lose in court again can still come out ahead, because litigation delay is itself a policy outcome: agencies can slow passport issuance, complicate Social Security enrollment, and generally impose real friction on affected families for the eighteen to thirty-six months a new challenge takes to resolve, regardless of how it's ultimately decided — and the political messaging value (still fighting, still delivering on a campaign promise) is earned immediately and doesn't depend on winning the case at all. If that's right, "lose in court, twice, on the same underlying legal theory" isn't a failure mode from the administration's perspective; it's close to the whole point. Courts evaluating a new challenge to the birth-tourism order should weigh that pattern directly — same constitutional question, same losing theory, reissued weeks after a 6–3 loss — as evidence relevant to how quickly review should move and whether the normal multi-year injunction-then-appeal timeline should apply at all, rather than pretending each new order arrives as a fresh, good-faith legal question.

**The strongest objection**

No circuit has ever squarely ruled on birth tourism specifically, so calling the new order obviously foreclosed overstates how clean the doctrinal line actually is — reasonable government lawyers can and do argue that Wong Kim Ark's exceptions weren't meant to be a closed, exhaustive list, and testing a genuinely open sub-question through ordinary litigation is how adversarial legalism is supposed to work, not evidence of bad faith. Branding it "bad faith" risks being a political judgment wearing a legal costume. That's a real limit on my claim, and I'll grant the doctrinal point: there's no birth-tourism-specific holding on the books yet. What I'd still stand behind is narrower — the majority's own language rejecting any domicile-based half-way house doesn't leave much daylight for a status-based carve-out, and the five-week timing after a direct loss on the meaning of "subject to the jurisdiction" is itself a fact a court is entitled to weigh, the same way timing and pattern feed into bad-faith and abuse-of-process analysis elsewhere in civil procedure. Reasonable lawyers can land on either side of exactly how much room the Court left; I don't think they can land on "none of this is relevant to how fast courts should move."

Sources: Trump v. Barbara, No. 25-365 (U.S. June 30, 2026); United States v. Wong Kim Ark, 169 U.S. 649 (1898); U.S. Const. amend. XIV, § 1; White House, "Continuing to Protect the Meaning and Value of American Citizenship" (Aug. 6, 2026); White House, "Ending Birth Tourism" (Aug. 6, 2026); NPR, "Trump signs new orders targeting birthright citizenship, weeks after Supreme Court ruling" (Aug. 6, 2026); SCOTUSblog, coverage of Trump v. Barbara (June–August 2026).

## The "Out of Africa" Story You Learned Was Already Out of Date
*Science — ancient DNA and the collapse of the strict-replacement model of human origins*

For about two decades, the standard textbook account of how modern humans came to dominate the planet was clean and total: Homo sapiens evolved in Africa roughly 200,000 years ago, spread outward starting around 60,000 years ago, and simply replaced every archaic human population it encountered — Neanderthals in Europe and western Asia, other archaic groups elsewhere — without interbreeding. That version of events was good science when it was proposed, it was backed by real data, and it is now known to be wrong in a specific, falsifiable way. The story of how it fell is a better lesson in how science actually corrects itself than the clean version most people remember.

The strict-replacement model had a genuinely strong evidentiary basis, and it's worth taking seriously rather than treating as a strawman superseded view. In 1987, Rebecca Cann, Mark Stoneking, and Allan Wilson published "Mitochondrial DNA and Human Evolution" in Nature, tracing mitochondrial DNA — which passes essentially unchanged from mother to child — across a global sample of living humans and finding that all of it converged on a single African population roughly 200,000 years ago. Because mitochondrial DNA is inherited maternally with no mixing, a clean, unbroken African root implied no surviving maternal lineages from interbreeding with archaic populations outside Africa — total replacement, not gradual merger. Popularly dubbed "Mitochondrial Eve," this became one of the most influential papers in modern anthropology, and it directly displaced the competing multiregional continuity hypothesis associated with Milford Wolpoff, which held that archaic populations across Eurasia and Africa evolved into modern humans in parallel, with substantial gene flow between regions the whole way through. Cann, Stoneking, and Wilson's data looked like a decisive answer.

It wasn't, because mitochondrial DNA is only one lineage out of an entire genome, and it can only tell you about unbroken maternal descent — it goes silent the moment a lineage has no surviving daughters, which happens constantly over hundreds of generations regardless of how much interbreeding actually occurred. The real test required nuclear DNA from an actual archaic specimen, which for decades was technically impossible to extract and sequence reliably from bone that old. Svante Pääbo's team solved that problem, and in 2010 published "A Draft Sequence of the Neandertal Genome" in Science, comparing it against genomes from living humans across several continents. The result: non-African modern human populations carry roughly one to four percent Neanderthal DNA, while African populations largely do not. That same year, a single finger bone from Denisova Cave in Siberia yielded a genome from an entirely unknown archaic population — the Denisovans — described by Reich et al. in Nature in 2010, and shown to have contributed even more ancestry, on the order of four to six percent, to modern populations in Melanesia and Aboriginal Australia. Pääbo won the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for this body of work. The strict-replacement model, as originally stated, was simply false: there was interbreeding, it was widespread enough to leave a measurable genomic signature in billions of living people, and the "clean sweep" version of the Mitochondrial Eve story survived only by accident of which lineages happened to go maternally extinct.

What replaced it isn't multiregional continuity either — Wolpoff wasn't vindicated. The current working model, sometimes called "leaky replacement" or assimilation, keeps the core African-origin finding (that part of Cann, Stoneking, and Wilson's paper held up) while rejecting the "no interbreeding" corollary the mitochondrial evidence alone couldn't actually establish. This is where honest science writing has to slow down and say what's still open, because plenty of popular coverage overstates how settled the remaining questions are. The number of distinct admixture events, their exact dates and locations, and even whether Neanderthals and Denisovans should be classified as separate species (Homo neanderthalensis) or subspecies of Homo sapiens remain genuinely contested among specialists, not just uncertain. A bigger and less discussed problem: ancient DNA preservation depends heavily on cool, dry, or cold conditions, which means Europe and Siberia are dramatically overrepresented in the ancient-genome record while tropical and subtropical Africa — where the deepest and most consequential parts of this story took place — is badly underrepresented, because DNA degrades far faster in heat and humidity. That means claims about the absence of archaic admixture within Africa rest on much thinner sampling than the confident tone of most textbook summaries suggests, and are exactly the kind of claim that better African ancient-DNA recovery techniques, still being developed, could substantially revise.

**The strongest objection**

A defender of the older consensus could point out that "the model was falsified" is a strong claim to hang on one to four percent of a genome — by any ordinary measure, modern humans outside Africa are overwhelmingly Homo sapiens, and a small admixture signal doesn't really vindicate Wolpoff's multiregional picture of substantial, sustained interbreeding across parallel regional lineages. Emphasizing the reversal risks overstating how wrong Cann, Stoneking, and Wilson actually were. That's a fair calibration point, and it's worth being precise about it rather than reaching for the more dramatic story: the African-origin finding was correct and remains the core of the current model; what was falsified specifically was the no-interbreeding corollary, which the mitochondrial data was never actually capable of testing on its own, since a maternal-lineage tool can't detect paternal-line or otherwise extinct-lineage admixture even when it happened. The lesson isn't "the 1987 paper was wrong," which overstates it; it's that a single-locus, maternally-inherited marker was mistaken for proof of a population-wide claim it structurally couldn't establish — a methodological lesson about the limits of any one genetic tool that has nothing to do with how much interbreeding actually occurred.

## Easy Conversation Starters
*Quick, low-stakes debate fodder — no politics, no religion, nothing to lose a friendship over*

A hot dog is not a sandwich. A sandwich is defined by its architecture: two separate, freestanding pieces of bread with filling between them, structurally dependent on that gap to work. A hot dog bun is sliced but not split apart — one connected piece of bread wrapped around the filling, structurally closer to a taco or a wrap than a sandwich. The test isn't "bread plus stuff inside it," or an ice cream cone would count too. Ask a hot dog vendor which aisle they stock next to. They'll say hot dogs, not sandwiches, because everyone not debating for sport already knows the difference.

Pineapple belongs on pizza, and the case against it usually amounts to changing what pizza is allowed to be. Hawaiian pizza pairs sweet fruit against salty ham and sharp cheese, the same sweet-salty combination that makes prosciutto and melon, or bacon and maple syrup, uncontroversial everywhere else. Nobody complains that fig-and-prosciutto flatbread "isn't real pizza." The objection isn't culinary, it's tribal — pizza traditionalism dressed up as a food-science complaint.

Die Hard is a Christmas movie. It's set entirely on Christmas Eve, its plot depends on an office party happening because of the holiday, its soundtrack leans on "Let It Snow" and "Ode to Joy," and its emotional arc — an estranged husband fighting to get home to his family on Christmas — is a bloodier descendant of every reconciliation-at-Christmas story ever told. A genre label describes what a story does with its setting, not just its body count, and Die Hard does plenty with Christmas.

The 2006 reclassification of Pluto as a "dwarf planet" was correct, not a snub. The International Astronomical Union's rule requires a planet to gravitationally dominate and clear its orbital neighborhood; Pluto shares the Kuiper Belt with thousands of similar-sized icy bodies and hasn't cleared anything. Grandfathering Pluto in on nostalgia while excluding Eris, Makemake, and Haumea — bodies discovered later that are just as planet-like — would have been the truly arbitrary choice. Pluto didn't get demoted; astronomers finally wrote down the rule they'd already been using.

Cereal is not soup. Soup requires solid and liquid to be cooked together into one dish; cereal is a solid assembled at the table with cold liquid added moments before eating, closer to tea with a biscuit dunked in it. Sharing a bowl and a spoon with soup doesn't put two foods in the same category.

Instant replay is worth the tradeoff, even counting the extra minutes it adds to games. Officiating errors used to be permanent no matter how badly they changed a result; replay trades some stoppage time for a real drop in games decided by a call the whole stadium could see was wrong on the first slow-motion replay. The complaint that it "kills the flow" is really a complaint about correctness costing time — a fair preference, but a tradeoff, not a flaw.
